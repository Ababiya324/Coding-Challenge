The Hidden Labor

Every day at CMU-Q, multilingual students engage in invisible labor before hitting "send" on emails to professors.

✎

They delete their authentic voice and replace it with something safer, more "academic."

⟷

Mid-sentence, a WhatsApp message from home pulls them into Arabic, then back to English.

⚙

They open ChatGPT to transform their natural expression into what they imagine professors expect.

∞

They continue switching between languages and multiple versions of themselves, realizing they are not "enough" in either context.

Key Concepts

Understanding the theoretical framework behind the invisible edit

Chronotopes

Blommaert, 2015

Distinct spatiotemporal frames that carry different norms, expectations, linguistic repertoires and forms of identities. Students navigate daily between home and university, each demanding different linguistic performances.

"Chronotopes invoke orders of indexicality valid in a specific timespace frame."

Enoughness

Blommaert & Varis, 2015

The socially constructed standard that students constantly calibrate against. Is their English "enough"? Their formality "enough"? Have they sufficiently erased markers of their autobiographical selves?

"Enoughness functions as a moving target, always subject to institutional micro-hegemonies."

Autobiographical Self

Ivanič, 1998

The writer's sense of their roots, the identity they bring to writing based on their life history. Students face constant pressure to suppress this self in favor of constructing a "discoursal self" conforming to academic standards.

"Writing is an act of identity in which people align themselves with socio-culturally shaped possibilities for self-hood."

Discoursal Self

Ivanič, 1998

The identity a writer constructs through discourse features. Writers construct discoursal selves "not out of an infinite range of possibilities, but out of the possibilities for self-hood which are supported by the socio-cultural and institutional context."

"The writer-as-performer attempting to create a writer-as-character recognizable to institutional gatekeepers."

Linguistic Shame

Hillman, 2022

The painful experience of feeling culturally inauthentic as speakers internalize institutional judgments about their linguistic inadequacy. What begins as external correction becomes internal self-censorship.

"Students internalize this judgment: their natural expression is wrong, their cultural markers are mistakes."

AI & Linguistic Homogeneity

Crawford, 2021

How AI systems like Grammarly and ChatGPT treat linguistic diversity as deviation rather than difference. Technology appears neutral but embeds institutional power, defining what counts as acceptable English.

"Systematically flagging cultural markers as mistakes."

Why This Matters

Beyond "Ordinary Formality"

Some may argue this experience reflects ordinary formality—that everyone performs when addressing authority. Yet formality involves adjusting tone while the autobiographical self remains intact. What this project documents is different: the systematic suppression of the autobiographical self to construct a discoursal self acceptable to institutional gatekeepers.

From Personal Failure to Structural Challenge

This community-wide pressure to perform "perfect Academic English" leads students to violently self-censor their authentic voices, yet the labor remains hidden, internalized as personal failure rather than recognized as a structural challenge created by English-medium instruction policies.

Making the Invisible Visible

Professors see only the final, "appropriate" email. They cannot see the fifteen minutes of deletion, the anxiety of word choice, the cultural markers systematically removed. Saying "students suppress their authentic voice" is easy to dismiss; watching someone select all, pause, and delete is not.
