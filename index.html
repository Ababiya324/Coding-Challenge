About This Project

Linguistic Identity in the Arabian Gulf

In the multilingual landscape of the Arabian Gulf, language is never neutral. For students at Carnegie Mellon University in Qatar, navigating between Arabic, English, and heritage languages creates complex moments of identity negotiation, pride, and sometimes shame.

This project maps these experiences across campus, revealing how institutional spaces become sites of linguistic policing The regulation and surveillance of language use, often resulting in feelings of inadequacy or shame about one's linguistic practices (Hillman, 2019), translanguaging The dynamic practices of multilingual speakers who fluidly move between languages and linguistic resources to communicate meaning, and resistance.

Key Academic Concepts

Linguistic Shame

Drawing on Sara Hillman's work, linguistic shame emerges when speakers internalize negative judgments about their language use, accent, or multilingual practices.

Chronotope

Following Blommaert, different campus spaces create distinct expectations for language use—what's acceptable in residence halls may be policed in academic buildings.

Translanguaging

The natural practice of multilingual speakers drawing on their full linguistic repertoire, challenging monolingual norms in institutional spaces.

Linguistic Enoughness

The struggle to be "enough" in any language—navigating judgments about being too Arabic, not Arabic enough, or having an "international accent" in English.

Methodology

Stories were collected through interviews, surveys, and ethnographic observation across CMU-Q campus. Student identities have been anonymized to protect privacy while preserving the authentic experiences shared.
